Cheapest item free

Buy three, get the cheapest one free.

Cheapest-item-free is the quantity offer with the most predictable cost. The shopper picks any three qualifying products and the cart discounts the lowest-priced of them to $0. Because the discount always lands on the cheapest line, your worst case is capped — unlike a flat percentage, which costs you most on your most expensive carts.

How cheapest-item-free works

1

Define the qualifying set. A collection, a tag, or specific products. Only items inside the set count toward the quantity and only they can be the free one.

2

Set the quantity. Buy 2, 3, or 4. Three is the common choice because it reads as generous without discounting half the cart.

3

The cart resolves the cheapest line. When the quantity is met, the lowest-priced qualifying item is discounted to $0 — recalculated live as the shopper changes the cart.

4

Repeat or cap. Decide whether a cart of six gets two free items or just one. Both are valid; only one matches your margin.

Why "cheapest free" beats a flat percentage

Compare two offers on a cart of three items at $20, $30, and $90. "Cheapest free" costs you $20 — 14% of the cart. "20% off three or more" costs you $28, and the more expensive the cart, the wider that gap grows. The percentage discount is cheapest where you least need help and most expensive where your margin was already healthiest in absolute terms.

Cheapest-free also reads better. "Free" is a categorically different word from "20% off" — it removes the mental arithmetic and it makes the third item feel like a decision about whether to accept a gift rather than a decision about price. That framing is why the mechanic survives in retail decade after decade.

The trade-off is self-selection. Shoppers quickly learn to add a cheap item to collect the discount, which is why the qualifying set matters more here than in any other offer type.

Designing the qualifying set

The qualifying set is your cost control. A set with a wide price spread invites gaming: if a $4 keychain qualifies alongside $90 jackets, every three-jacket cart will acquire a keychain and nothing will be free that you minded giving.

  • Keep the spread narrow. Aim for a set where the cheapest item is no less than roughly 60% of the most expensive. Price bands, not whole catalogs.
  • Exclude the loss leaders. Anything already discounted, clearance, or sold near cost does not belong in the set.
  • Use it to clear a category. A set drawn from one collection turns the offer into inventory movement with a known cost per unit.
  • Watch variants. If a product has a $12 and a $40 variant, the rule sees two different prices. Qualify at the variant level when price varies within a product.

Repeat behaviour and the six-item cart

The question every merchant hits in week one: a shopper adds six qualifying items — do they get one free or two? If the offer repeats, your cost scales linearly with cart size and large carts become your least profitable orders. If it does not repeat, your most enthusiastic shoppers feel short-changed at exactly the moment they were buying the most.

The usual resolution is to let it repeat but cap it — two applications per order, say — which keeps the large cart feeling rewarded without an open-ended liability. Whichever you choose, state it in the cart copy. "Cheapest of every 3 free" and "cheapest of your first 3 free" are both honest; only one matches your rule.

Where it fits against other quantity offers

Cheapest-free, BOGO, and volume discounts all reward quantity, and they compete for the same slot in the shopper's attention. A rough guide: use BOGO when you want a specific second product moved, cheapest-free when you want quantity without dictating which products, and volume discounts when the same SKU is bought in multiples.

Running two of them at once on overlapping collections produces carts where the rules disagree. Offer priority rules settle which fires, and you should set them before launch rather than after the first confused support email.

Frequently asked questions

Can Shopify do buy 3 get the cheapest free natively?

Shopify's Buy X Get Y automatic discount can discount a specified Y product, but it does not resolve "whichever item in the cart is cheapest" across a qualifying set, and it will not recalculate as the shopper edits the cart. That dynamic resolution is what an app adds.

What stops shoppers adding a cheap item to get a discount?

The qualifying set. Keep the price spread inside it narrow and exclude low-priced or already-discounted products, and the cheapest qualifying line stays close to the rest of the cart in value.

Does the discount apply more than once in a big cart?

That is configurable. Repeating scales your cost with cart size; not repeating can feel stingy on large carts. Capping repeats at two per order is a common middle path. Whatever you choose, say it in the cart copy.

How is the cheapest item decided?

By the line price of qualifying items at the moment the cart is evaluated, recalculated whenever the cart changes. If a product's variants differ in price, qualify at the variant level.

Can I run it on a single collection only?

Yes — and you usually should. A collection-scoped offer turns the mechanic into targeted inventory movement with a predictable cost per unit.

Does it work with other discounts?

It depends on what else is running and on Shopify's combination rules for the discount types involved. See discount stacking for which combinations Shopify permits, and set an explicit offer priority so only one quantity offer fires per cart.
